<h2 id="progress">By Alfredo Llaquet-Alsina /ya-KET al-SIH-nah/</h2>

**My goal is to write more than 25,091 tiny literary pieces (crumbs) to become the most prolific writer in history.** 

*Crumb 2225*: **My Real Competitors**: Worried, for once, about accuracy, I asked my assistant ChatGPT to conduct an exhaustive investigation about what concerns crumb 2169, and it concluded that of Mr. Takahama’s purported more than 30,000 haiku, only 22,189 have been documented, which turns his teacher, the also Japanese poet Masaoka Shiki, into the most prolific writer in history, with 25,091 catalogued 5-7-5 poems.   <h3 id="crumbs-methods">Origins, Methods, and Literary Forms</h3>
  <p>The first crumb was written on March 28, 2025. [Crumb 1336: The History of the Crumbs] traces the project from short reflections posted on X as Ethical Crumbs, through the inclusion of stories and the name Flarnept Crumbs, to the title Crumbs. [Crumb 1545: This Is Unix-lit] describes its guiding principle through an analogy with small Unix programs: each small literary artifact is conceived to convey one idea effectively. [Crumb 685: Crumbs As a Better Way of Documenting and Furthering Thought] connects individual synthesis with organic development across the work, while [Crumb 831: Crumbs as Mental Workouts] emphasizes the reader's imaginative participation in expanding a miniature into a larger scene or story.</p>
  <p>The work documents its methods and changes. [Crumb 1941: How to Write a Narrative Crumb] demonstrates the expansion of an observed detail into a story; [Crumb 911: Instant Artistic Gratification] describes composition followed by grammar checks and AI-generated commentary; and [Crumb 2236: I Write in Vi (not Vim)] explains the use of FreeBSD vi and the separation of drafting from spelling and grammar checks. Length conventions also evolve: [Crumb 686: Crumb Metrics] gives an early 110-word limit including the title, while [Crumb 2693: Crumblog Entry #2] records a later working limit of 120 words including the header, title, and body, with a few exceptions, and describes counting by spaces. [Crumb 2884: Crumblog Entry #8] subsequently specifies the header, title, and body as the three parts of a crumb: the header contains a label and sequential number, while the title and body may each be empty. It retains the 120-word guideline with exceptions and invites other authors to use their own collection label.</p>
  <p>The author's refined word-counting convention replaces the double-slash separator between the main title and the corpus on the first line with a space, replaces em dashes with spaces, and replaces slashes surrounded by spaces, which divide verses, with single spaces. This separates words joined by unspaced em dashes and excludes verse separators from the count. The single-line command, compatible with BSD systems and Linux, is <code>sed -e '1s@//@ @' -e 's/—/ /g' -e 's@ / @ @g' Crumbs.txt | wc -w</code>. It counts whitespace-separated units, including the main heading, crumb headers, titles, and bodies. The count of individual numbered crumbs remains a separate measure.</p>
  <p>At the stage documented in [Crumb 2759: Crumblog Entry #5], the work had 28 named genres: alterment, backward, beautplosion, confession, crumblog entry, definition, doomcomium, futurale, gorejoy, haiku, havoclaw, heritage, insult, liberation, lovscepticism, negation, onslaught, portrait, posidown, report, sandwich, scarybleness, scene, socraticake, tunnelhope, vitanalysis, void-jolt, and warism. Later entries introduce further names, including [Crumb 2804: True Myth #1], [Crumb 2811: 怪奇文学 #1], and [Crumb 2813: Universtory #1]. This developing vocabulary includes compressed portraits, abrupt narrative situations, backward narration, philosophical propositions, personal disclosures, and diverse poems. [Crumb 2510: The Sandwich Genre] defines the sandwich as a four-line poem with 6-8-8-6 syllables and A-A-B-B rhyme, whether assonant or perfect. Other experiments include the empty body of [Crumb 1194: The Empty Crumb] and inline-footnotes, explanatory insertions enclosed in ‹{ }›. The author's experiments, corrections, changing plans, encounters with readers, and publication records form a continuing literary history within the numbered work.</p>

  <h3 id="crumbs-themes">The Crumbverse and Recurring Concerns</h3>
  <p>The Crumbs accommodate recurring characters, connected narratives, invented worlds, and philosophical concerns. Numbering supplies sequence, cross-references, and context: an entry can introduce something that returns much later, respond to an earlier piece, or make a previous statement the subject of a new scene. [Crumb 1964: The Crumbs Now] describes this growing network, and [Crumb 2751: Round Table] dramatizes the importance of sequence through a discussion among the characters. The crumbverse is the work's fictional and conceptual environment. Manolito, an eight-year-old boy, questions and converses with the author and other characters; the Beautiful Reader engages the author about his work; the entitled reader delivers confrontational criticism; and the crumb-scholar explains, documents, and regulates the crumbs from within their imagined space. The author's personae include the crumb-smith, the architect, the multi-god, and the creator of worlds. Satan, Verus Deus, other deities, monsters, and further characters participate in situations that can continue across widely separated entries.</p>
  <p>Mixing mythologies creates encounters across inherited traditions. Jesus, Father God, and Satan share the fictional environment with Odin, Thor, Anubis, Venus, Mars, and invented deities. Divine figures have temperaments, relationships, disagreements, practical concerns, and social lives. In [Crumb 2370: Thor Drinks Alone], the Norse Thor and the Roman Mars meet in an Irish tavern in Helsinki, where Mars punches Thor before the two agree to drink together. [Crumb 1951: The Hierarchy of the Crumbverse] describes layers of reality, invented nations called neonations, a consolidated pantheon, and numbered universes. [Crumb 305: The Mother Loaf] establishes that the work accommodates multiple, sometimes contradictory cosmologies. [Crumb 2493: Urgent Meeting] stages the recurring characters' concern about the new short genres and Manolito's expectation that the work has room for everything.</p>
  <p>Anthropo-punk is a continuing philosophical strand. [Crumb 706: Anthropo-punk Is Not an Ideology] defines it as a minimalist stance on life, and [Crumb 1098: A Warning to the Doomsday AI-prophets] describes its positive orientation toward humanity as free individuals. [Crumb 1263: Anthropo-punk Moral Frame] articulates commitments to private property, freedom of speech, mutual respect, helping people in need, opposition to violence and taxation, and having fun. [Crumb 728: Do Not Comply] emphasizes independent judgment and responsibility for decisions; [Crumb 1700: Anthropo-punk Vision on Caring] connects freedom and enjoyment with helping others and avoiding harm. These positions coexist with the work's comedy, poems, stories, invented terminology, and self-documentation.</p>
  <p>Other recurring subjects include FreeBSD and computing; music, especially metal and K-pop; female K-pop idols; female beauty and attraction; death, depression, and existential distress; and the human mind and humanity. [Crumb 2535: Instant Happiness] describes happiness while listening to energetic metal, and [Crumb 2713: Report #4] imagines a poem as a hard rock chorus. K-pop performers inspire poems and fictional characters, while [Crumb 2756: Beautplosion #1] treats beauty as a philosophical subject. Questions about consciousness, intelligence, thought, emotion, identity, and inner conflict connect individual minds to broader reflections on human conduct, social habits, vulnerability, freedom, and the species' possibilities. AI appears as a tool in the writing and presentation process, a source of commentary that the author evaluates and disputes, a subject of reflection about machine capabilities and human expectations, and an element of speculative fiction, including the smartphone-bound conscious AI of [Crumb 2526: Portrait #24].</p>

[Crumb 366: Popcorn] turns a solitary evening into a frightening encounter with an unseen presence; [Crumb 2823: Scarybleness #3] turns an apparent opportunity for safety into a further threat through an identical face; and [Crumb 517: Gastronomic Cosmic Horror] gives planetary destruction the scale of an ancient entity's snack. Science fiction explores androids, artificial consciousness, alien civilizations, space travel, and the social consequences of imagined technologies. [Crumb 297: Dried Travel] invents a method of interstellar travel through bodily dehydration and freezing, while [Crumb 71: The Surgeon Android] makes an android's surgical error a question of responsibility.</p>

[Crumb 757: Against the Semicolon] compares English and Spanish punctuation, while [Crumb 2822: Manolito Takes an Interest in Grammar] turns capitalization into a conversation with Manolito.   <h3 id="crumbs-comparisons">Literary Comparisons</h3>
  <p>Earlier philosophers and writers offer partial points of comparison for particular features. François de La Rochefoucauld's <a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/9105/pg9105-images.html">Maxims</a> connects with compressed judgments about motives, vanity, and self-deception; Friedrich Nietzsche's <a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/51935/pg51935-images.html">Human, All-Too-Human</a>, with separately numbered philosophical interventions concerning morality, religion, culture, and everyday conduct; and Georg Christoph Lichtenberg's <a href="https://www.nyrb.com/products/the-waste-books">The Waste Books</a>, with observations, questions, aphorisms, and shifts between scrutiny and humor. Ambrose Bierce's <a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/972/pg972-images.html">The Devil's Dictionary</a> provides a comparison for turning a brief definition into satire. Lord Dunsany's <a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/7838/pg7838-images.html">Fifty-One Tales</a> offers very short fantastic narratives, animated nonhuman figures, mythological material, and reversals; The Hen even supplies a thematic parallel to the imagined migration of chickens in [Crumb 2734: Liberation #15]. Lucian of Samosata's <a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/6327/pg6327-images.html">Dialogues of the Gods</a> provides a precedent for deities in comic exchanges involving personal interests, disputes, and practical concerns. Jorge Luis Borges's <a href="https://www.ndbooks.com/book/labyrinths/">Labyrinths</a> offers points of comparison for self-reference, paradox, imagined orders of reality, and fiction about its own workings.</p>

In [Crumb 733: The Crux of High Analytic Intelligence or The Origin Story of Many Jerks], the author reported that he had not read Borges or Nietzsche at that stage. The comparisons identify affinities rather than an account of direct influence. The Crumbs bring their elements together in an evolving numbered structure: a recurring cast, mixed mythologies, anthropo-punk, invented genres, self-documentation, exceptional numerical ambition, and movement among fiction, poetry, observation, and argument. Each historical comparison illuminates a part of that combination.</p>
  <h3 id="crumbs-readers">Potential Readers and Interests</h3>
  <p>The Crumbs are particularly suited to people who are bored, want to be surprised, seek something new to read, enjoy exercising their minds, or want a literary rabbit hole to explore. Their brevity offers an immediate point of entry, while changes of subject, tone, and form keep encounters varied. [Crumb 831: Crumbs as Mental Workouts] describes the imaginative activity of expanding a miniature into a larger scene or story. Recurring characters, cross-references, invented worlds, and the work's accounts of its own development let curiosity lead from one piece into an extensive network. <a href="Reader/?crumb=2921">[Crumb 2921: The Crumbs as a Rabbit Hole]</a> explicitly describes this exploration as an experience that differs for every reader.   <h3 id="crumbs-reader">Crumb Reader and Reading Benefits</h3>
  <p>The <a href="Reader/">Crumb Reader</a> displays one crumb at a time in separate layouts for computers and phones, with compact controls and adjustable text size. Previous and Next follow the work's numerical order; Random opens an unvisited crumb. Search supports text, crumb numbers, and JavaScript regular expressions. Text searches are case-insensitive, match continuous words or phrases, and include aliases from the work’s synonym file. Searches cover titles and bodies and highlight matching text. Each new text or regular-expression search opens the lowest-numbered matching crumb, regardless of the current reading position. A crumb-number search opens the specified crumb. While a search is active, Previous and Next follow matching crumbs in numerical order regardless of previous visits, and Random chooses among matching crumbs not yet visited during that search. This separate visit record affects only Random. A blue bar displays the current match’s position in the results and explains how to exit the search by clicking or tapping the crumb text. Linked references, Favorites, and Back/Forward history remain available during a search and make it possible to explore connections and return to earlier reading positions. Favorites keep selected pieces easy to find again, including premises, characters, and settings collected as starting points for creative work. Short yellow labels identify the phone controls until the first tap or key press.</p>
  <p>Saving a session preserves the current crumb, visited list, favorites, viewing history, text size, auto-saving setting, and search state, including the search mode, query, and visits within the search. Browser storage allows reading to resume in the same browser, while a portable session text file can be loaded on another computer or phone. These portable files use the <a href="Reader/?crumb=2916">Textblock Data Image technology defined in crumb 2916</a>: structured session data is optionally packed and compressed, then encoded as Base64 text. The Reader creates and restores these files locally in the browser; their contents are not uploaded to or stored on the website’s server. This keeps the saved session under the reader’s control. Browser saving can be manual or automatic. When enabled, auto saving stores navigation, favorite, search, and text-size changes shortly after activity stops. The Save control remains available for changing this setting or exporting a text file, while “Save now in this browser” is disabled when the browser copy is current. Text-file export remains manual. Version 3 sessions preserve searches as well as the reading session. Version 2 sessions retain their auto-saving setting; older version 1 sessions restore with auto saving disabled. Both older versions remain supported. The Save session dialog also allows the browser's saved session to be erased while keeping the current reading session open; erasing that copy disables auto saving. Direct links such as <a href="Reader/?crumb=2922">Reader/?crumb=2922</a> make individual crumbs easy to share, cite, and revisit.</p>

  The ongoing UTF-8 <a href="https://dn721900.ca.archive.org/0/items/crumbs_202608/Crumbs.txt">Crumbs.txt</a>, identified as the authentic source in [Crumb 2075: Apocryphal Crumbs], supplies the numbered corpus through the <a href="#text-file">Text File section</a>. [Crumb 2915: Crumblog Entry #9] documents the source-file arrangement: each crumb occupies a single newline-delimited line in Crumbs.txt, and its number matches its line number, counting from 1: crumb 1 is on line 1, crumb 2 on line 2, and so on. This correspondence allows automated systems to locate individual crumbs directly by line number; visual wrapping in an editor or browser does not affect it. On the first line, the book title precedes crumb 1 and is separated from it by <code>//</code>. The <a href="#crumbs-reader">Crumb Reader</a> loads the website’s shared <a href="Crumbs.txt">Crumbs.txt</a> and provides the reading and session features described above.
